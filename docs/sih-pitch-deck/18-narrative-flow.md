# 18 — Narrative flow

Six slides, one argument. Each transition must create a reason to care about the next slide.

---

## The argument in one line

**There's an inference problem here that the obvious approach can't solve → we built the system
that solves it → here's proof it works and proof we know its limits → here's who benefits → here's
the ground truth underneath.**

## The mapping problem, solved

The classic pitch arc is *Problem → Why existing is insufficient → Our approach → How → Proof →
Impact*. The official template has **no problem slide and no competition slide**. So the arc is
compressed:

| Classic beat | Where it actually lives |
|---|---|
| Problem | Slide 2, under *"How it addresses the problem"* — 2 lines |
| Why existing approaches fall short | Slide 2, under *"Innovation and uniqueness"* — 1 line |
| Our approach | Slide 2 headline + Slide 3 |
| How it works | Slide 3 (the mandatory flow chart) |
| Proof | Slide 4 |
| Impact | Slide 5 |
| Grounding | Slide 6 |

**Slide 2 carries three classic beats at once.** That is why it is the hardest slide to design and
the one that must not be diluted.

## Transitions

**1 → 2.** *"SIH26159, NTRO, Software."* → The judge now needs to know what the thing is. Slide 2
opens with the answer, not with background.

**2 → 3.** Slide 2 ends on a claim: *cross-session reasoning, absent from five audited
implementations.* The judge's next thought is **"is that actually built, or is it a concept?"** —
Slide 3 exists to answer exactly that. The pipeline diagram is the response to a question Slide 2
deliberately provokes.

**3 → 4.** Slide 3 shows an engineered system. The judge's next thought is **"does it work on
anything real?"** — Slide 4 opens with 1234 tests and 10 real vendor captures. The transition is
from *architecture* to *evidence*.

**4 → 5.** Slide 4 ends on honestly-stated limits. Having earned trust, the judge now asks **"so
who is this actually for?"** — Slide 5 answers with PS-named users and operational benefits. The
limitation slide is what makes the benefit slide believable; in the reverse order, benefits read
as marketing.

**5 → 6.** Slide 5 makes operational claims. The judge's last question is **"grounded in
what?"** — Slide 6 answers with 11 standards and the research corpus. The deck closes on
authority, not on aspiration.

## The deliberate inversion

Most decks put limitations last, apologetically, or omit them. We put them at the **centre of
Slide 4**, where the official template explicitly asks for *"potential challenges and risks"*.

This is the deck's strategic core: **stating D-11 and A-02 precisely, with their mitigations,
converts the biggest credibility risk into the strongest credibility signal** — and it does so in
the one place the template already reserves for it. A judge who sees a team name its own limits
accurately trusts every other number on the page.

## What each slide leaves the judge thinking

| After | The judge thinks |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Right problem statement, properly submitted." |
| 2 | "They found an inference problem the obvious approach can't solve." |
| 3 | "This is engineered, and the AI boundary is deliberate." |
| 4 | "It runs, it's measured, and they know exactly what it can't prove." |
| 5 | "This fits a real analyst workflow." |
| 6 | "Standards-grounded, not hand-waved." |

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Slide 2 becomes a feature list** → the differentiator drowns. One idea only.
- **Slide 3 becomes an architecture museum** → one pipeline, one evidence strip, stop.
- **Slide 4 becomes apologetic** → limitations are stated as engineering facts with mitigations,
  never as regrets.
- **Slide 5 invents numbers** → instant credibility collapse; qualitative impact is enough.
- **Slide 6 pads references** → cite only standards the rules actually use.
